Q1. Mean, median and skewness

The mean is misleading because the few deliveries above 90 minutes are extreme values that pull the mean upward. 
The median is a better measure of typical delivery time because it is less affected by outliers. 
The distribution is right-skewed (positively skewed) because most deliveries are around 25–30 minutes while a 
small number have very large values.

Q2. Bayes' Theorem

Given:

Prior probability = 5% = 0.05
Probability of detecting fraud = 90% = 0.90
False-positive rate = 8% = 0.08
$$ P(Fraud|Flagged)=\frac{0.90\times0.05}{(0.90\times0.05)+(0.08\times0.95)} $$ $$ =\frac{0.045}{0.121}=0.3719 $$

Answer = approximately 37.19%.

Although the model is 90% accurate at detecting fraudulent orders, most orders are legitimate, so false positives 
make up a significant proportion of flagged orders.

Q3. Preprocessing problems

Missing restaurant ratings: Fill the missing values using an appropriate imputation technique, such as the mean or
median. Missing values can cause inconsistent training information and reduce model performance.

Categorical text features: Apply one-hot encoding to payment_method and cuisine_type. 
Machine-learning models cannot directly use raw text categories effectively. 
Encoding converts them into numerical features.

Q4. Imbalanced classification

Overall accuracy is misleading because only 1% of orders are fraudulent. 
A model could achieve very high accuracy by mostly predicting "not fraud."

Model B detects:

$$ \frac{420}{500}\times100=84\% $$

So Model B has an 84% recall for fraudulent orders.

Recall is the appropriate metric because the finance team's priority is minimizing missed fraud:

$$ Recall=\frac{TP}{TP+FN} $$

Therefore, the finance team should choose Model B, because it catches 420 of the 500 fraudulent orders.

Q5. K-Means vs DBSCAN

I would recommend K-Means because the customers naturally form 3–4 groups with similar density and there are 
very few outliers. K-Means is efficient for a large dataset of 80,000 customers and works well when the desired
groups are relatively compact. A limitation is that the number of clusters must be selected in advance, and 
K-Means may not handle irregularly shaped clusters well.

Q6. PCA before K-Means

PCA reduces the 35 features into a smaller number of principal components while retaining as much important 
variance as possible. This can make K-Means faster, reduce the effect of highly correlated features, and make 
visualization possible.

The trade-off is that retaining fewer components can lose information. The explained variance ratio can be used 
to choose enough components to retain an acceptable percentage of the original variance.
